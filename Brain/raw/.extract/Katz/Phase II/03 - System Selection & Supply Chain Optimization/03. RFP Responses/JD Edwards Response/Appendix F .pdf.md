Production and Distribution Planning

The Issue: Tactical Planning within a Complex Supply Chain

Successful companies no longer compete as individual entities. Instead, they act as part of complex
supply chain networks. Within these networks, partners must be able to easily share information,
operate from a single, optimized plan, and quickly react to unforeseen events.

Tactical planning within a complex supply chain represents one of the most difficult challenges.
Traditional tools such as master production scheduling (MPS), capacity requirements planning (CRP),
and distribution requirements planning (DRP) aren’t up to the task. These tools limit your supply
chain effectiveness by producing unconstrained plans that function at a single enterprise level, at best.
Furthermore, they impede rapid decision making because they are not exception-driven, event-based,
or enabled for collaboration.

Constrained planning, information sharing, and event management are key elements of a competitive
supply chain network. Optimized procurement, manufacturing, and distribution plans must be generated
in collaboration with customers and suppliers. Once implemented, these plans must be immediately
visible to those responsible for execution, thereby synchronizing the many people, processes, and
systems that traditionally operate as disparate functional units.

The Solution: Production and Distribution Planning

It helps optimize the day-to-day flow of goods from supplier to manufacturer, manu-

J.D. Edwards Production and Distribution Planning is a tactical planning tool for the entire supply
chain network.
facturer to distributor, and distributor to customer — fully replacing outdated MPS, CRP, and DRP
processes within your enterprise. You are able to produce plans that respect constraints within your
supply chain, and which are optimized around your key cost drivers.

Synchronizing costs and constraints, time-phased procurement, production, and distribution plans are
calculated simultaneously. You avoid procurement plans that bring in more inventory than manufac-
turing can use, or production plans that leave excess inventory sitting in trucks. The resulting plans
