Vendor Rating: JDA Software Group

JDA is doing well in a tough market by selling more applications to its customers in retail
and manufacturing. But its portfolio is a patchwork of different technologies, which can
make it hard to implement and support.

JDA Software Group, Inc Overall Rating: Promising

acquisitions. These acquisitions have left JDA with a very large installed base and huge potential for
cross-selling. But a disparate technology assembly is creating challenges for the company. JDA’s focus
on existing customers will help it understand that it needs to integrate these acquisitions into a seamless,
flexible solution, and that technology is only one aspect of applications integration. Companies that are

Corporate Viability: JDA Software Group (JDA) is the most global vendor of business applications
software in the distribution industry, with the exception of SAP. JDA was founded in Canada in 1979 as a
pure-play merchandising management vendor. The company provides a suite of solutions to the retail
and distribution market. It operates from 36 offices in 20 countries and services clients in more than 60
nations.

JDA proved itself
to be a visionary by anticipating the need to provide merchandise management,
optimization and collaboration solutions globally through a broad retail and distribution suite. But eight
acquisitions in five years have meant that building the suite carries challenges that make the execution
more difficult, like having to use solutions running on disparate technologies. The main challenges are in
technology and integration as clients — especially Tier 2 retailers (see Note 1) — become more willing to
buy an integrated solution from a single vendor.

Note 1

Tier 1 retailers have revenue of more than $5 billion. There are fewer than 100 worldwide.

Tier 2 retailers have between $100 million and $5 billion in revenue. There are more than 3,000
worldwide.

JDA claims the broadest product suite in the retail market. It also maintains that every product in its
portfolio can be implemented as a stand-alone solution and that its strategy is to keep it this way. JDA
wants to be able to address whatever tactical problem a customer may have, to ensure a rapid return on
investment (ROI).

Market Offerings: A Broad Yet Loosely Integrated Product Offering

Customers often struggle to understand the breadth and depth of JDA’s solutions. JDA originally offered
a merchandise management product running on IBM AS/400. It extended its functional scope into the
collaborative replenishment planning area with the acquisition of E3, a well-known and widely deployed
stand-alone AS/400 solution. With this acquisition, JDA recognized the need for business-to-business
processes and collaboration between manufacturers and retailers.

JDA Portfolio is a patchwork of more than 50 products on different platforms and technologies (see Table
3). Users willing to deploy a broad solution from JDA Portfolio should make sure that the considered
products work seamlessly together. Recent releases of JDA Portfolio are starting to take care of
integration between the products but completion will take several years.

Technology

JDA has some of the most recognized products in each of the stand-alone application areas. Its goal is
not to have a single technology but to deliver an architecture where products on different platforms can sit
above an integration framework. Commitment to Microsoft.NET was made public to customers in May
2003, but no firm availability dates were provided other than that delivery would start during 2004.

Integration between different pieces of the solution is still one of the major concerns for users and
prospects and it is often unclear what functionality sits with what product. This means that JDA does not
communicate its integration strategy clearly at the application or functional level.

Pricing

JDA’s suite is so broad that it includes products in all price ranges. JDA has long been considered a mid-
market player (as opposed to Retek, which had a clear focus on Tier 1 retailers), but it has seen its
average deal size increasing in the past 18 months. This comes from more cross-selling. For example,

With JDA’s cross-selling strategy for products in different technologies, customers should be aware that
total cost of ownership could easily offset lower pricing, compared with a more integrated solution like
SAP for Retail. There is no evidence that JDA has had to lower its prices when competing against SAP in
retail, which shows that users recognize and value JDA’s expertise more highly than pure product
integration.

Customer Service/Support: Sales/Distribution

Over half of JDA’s revenue comes from established customers. JDA knows that it must sell more
products to its installed base, as well as find new customers to increase its sources of revenue. JDA’s
focus is on demonstrating, at a country level, that small projects can pay off rapidly. JDA understands
that this is vital for building a sustainable presence in many countries where retailers are still reluctant to
trust vendors of packaged software. The strategy is to generate extreme customer satisfaction, and then
sell by reference. But JDA still has work to do on support to be able to sell by reference alone.

Support/Account Management

Satisfaction levels are lower for users with several products, although we have spoken to many satisfied
customers that use stand-alone products. It is not uncommon for customers deploying JDA products
across a process to have had to figure out which products to buy from JDA and how to assemble them.
This will become more difficult to do because the JDA Portfolio is so broad.

JDA must invest in training cross-application experts that will be able to provide answers and support to
customers, across all products. This improvement in professional services applies to JDA internally and to
its partners. Companies should monitor JDA’s improvements in this area as an indicator of
its
competitiveness in the retail applications market.
