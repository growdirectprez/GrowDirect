Enthusiasm Propels Personalization

eCommerce managers expect personalization to make their sites easier

to use and to boost sales. Most interviewees have a gut feeling that their

efforts are succeeding, even though few measure results. As they deploy a

variety of solutions, managers struggle to craft rules that determine what

content to deliver and how to make the technology work.

Sites Must Step Up To Smart Personalization

Although our interviewees are bullish on their personalization efforts,

most lack the clear goals, supporting infrastructure, and robust technology

to deliver value to site visitors. As a result, their customers receive a

disappointing payoff. To realize personalization(cid:146)s potential, sites must

build an architecture that recognizes individuals, and then customizes

content, sales, and service to their needs. Merchants must integrate

best-of-breed applications to accomplish these goals.

REALITY CONTRADICTS OPTIMISM
Interviewee enthusiasm about customized offerings clashes with reality as
personalization efforts fail to overcome today(cid:146)s unimpressive 2% look-to-buy ratios.
In fact, retailer optimism fades over time -- inexperienced site managers think that
personalization will solve their customer retention problems, but experienced managers
believe the answer lies in better service (see the June 1999 Forrester Report (cid:147)Making
Net Shoppers Loyal(cid:148)). The reason is that today(cid:146)s sites fail either to understand
customers or to actively perform services for them -- or both (see Figure 2). This
misguided personalization wastes customers(cid:146) time and annoys them as:

(cid:149) Lazy personalizers don(cid:146)t leverage their data. Knowing a customer(cid:146)s allergies
should help an online drugstore to screen out pain relievers that might trigger a
reaction. Unfortunately, even after we entered aspirin into an allergy profile at
PlanetRx, the site promoted -- and let us buy -- Aleve, a product that warns away
those with allergies to aspirin.

(cid:149) Trivial personalizers underwhelm. The New York Life, General Motors, and
Fleet Bank sites lure visitors into volunteering their preferences and personal
information in return for tailored content. But instead of a customized insurance
profile, personal auto maintenance suggestions, or specific financial advice, these
companies insult customers with (cid:147)tailored(cid:148) menus of marketing messages.

(cid:149) Overeager personalizers make faulty assumptions. Even the big guns can
stumble by leaping to a bad conclusion based on inadequate information --
Outpost.com cuts its margins by recommending the $19.95 surge protector after
customers have already put the $59.95 version in their shopping carts.

Companies Lose Revenues
The result of these misguided efforts is that sites leave money on the table. Ineffective
personalizers reap three painful results:

(cid:149) Poor customer retention. When personalization misfires, customers never

return to view their uncompelling customized content. Their next trip back to the
site comes when they show up to turn off the annoying opt-in email that never
delivered value but did clog up their mailboxes with daily generic offers.

(cid:149) Lower margins. Unable to satisfy customer needs with value-added services,
companies like Sears and Service Merchandise must lower prices to compete
against comparison engines, auctions, and aggregators.

(cid:149) Lost sales. Most companies only convert 2% of lookers to buyers -- or less.

Despite energies spent on simplifying navigation, speeding checkout, reducing
first-time buying risk, and enhancing loyalty with incentives, many customers still
just kick the tires and leave.

DEFINING SMART PERSONALIZATION
To stem lost revenue opportunities and build loyalty with customers, companies
must enhance sites by providing smart personalization. Forrester defines smart
personalization as:

Content and services actively tailored to individuals based on rich knowledge about
their preferences and behavior.

Smart personalization combines two fundamental components to produce value for
customers:

(cid:149) Rich customer knowledge provides insight. A holistic profile allows firms to

understand what customers want and need. This understanding enables companies
to intelligently anticipate desirable products and services -- and avoid embarrassing
gaffes. Garden.com uses its profiles to warn customers when a plant they are about
to buy won(cid:146)t grow well in their climate.

(cid:149) Actively tailored content and services deliver a payoff. Rather than passively
parroting preferences back to customers, compelling personalizers act on data to
provide higher levels of service. After visitors enter the make, model, and year of
their cars, CarPoint(cid:146)s (cid:147)Personal Auto Page(cid:148) provides Kelly Blue Book values,
maintenance suggestions, and recall alerts (see Figure 3).

SITES MUST CREATE A PERSONALIZATION INFRASTRUCTURE
To move from disappointing initiatives to smart personalization, firms need to step up
their efforts in three major areas:

1) Tagging content. To deliver information and services that are highly relevant to
an individual, firms must label their content with enough details to identify value
while eliminating background noise.

2) Creating a unified profile. Firms must tap existing customer data from retail

and direct marketing channels, collect behavioral information from Web sites, and
exchange value in return for a visitor(cid:146)s personal information and preferences.

3) Implementing matchmaking engines. Merchandising rules and dynamic
algorithms bring users together with the appropriate content and services.

1) Tagging Content
Basic content tagging has become commonplace. However, smart personalization
demands a higher level of effort -- detailed metadata that is both granular and
hierarchical (see the May 1999 Forrester Report (cid:147)On-line Retail Data Strategies(cid:148)).

2) Creating A Unified Profile
Today, customer data is scattered throughout the enterprise in Balkanized applications
owned by organizations that collect and hoard information (see the June 1999 Forrester
Report (cid:147)The Demise of CRM(cid:148)). This means that customers must reenter information as
they move between sections of a site -- or be forgotten. Smart personalizers will avoid
this problem when they:

(cid:149) Pull together dispersed customer records. To create a holistic customer view

across applications and channels, Web owners must create a customer data model
that includes transaction, service, preference, and group data. Business intelligence
tools from firms like Personify, RightPoint, Andromedia, and net.Genesis add
real-time context to the historic data, giving companies a better idea of the
customer(cid:146)s current needs.

(cid:149) Trade value for new data. Companies can trick users into providing information
by offering sweepstakes and gimmicks like personal home pages. However, once
the promotion is over, so is the customer relationship. In contrast, companies that
provide customers with valuable timesaving and problem-solving services will
gather a steady flow of personal data. Netcenter collects geographic information
by providing weather and local event updates, financial information by tracking
and analyzing portfolios, and birth dates by offering horoscopes.

(cid:149) Put users in control of their profiles. Users must feel in control of their

personal information and be able to change, add, delete, and undo items. This
creates a virtuous cycle in which customers feel safe volunteering more about
themselves, receive more value in return, and have a strong motive to keep data
up-to-date. Coldwell Banker(cid:146)s Personal Retriever takes this approach by allowing
users to save and edit multiple real estate profiles against which it performs
ongoing housing searches.

3) Implementing Matchmaking Engines
Once site managers understand both their customers and content, they can move to
smart personalization by instilling business logic that brings the two together.

(cid:149) Build business rules for known events. Where business logic is apparent, firms

must develop a set of rules for interacting with customers -- such as lowering
prices for best customers when inventory is high. For example, MaxMiles(cid:146)
MileageMiner uses simple rules to help customers find frequent-flier-mile
earning opportunities in locations that they travel to frequently.

Three Functions Support A Unified Profile
A unified customer profile must combine real-time data collection with disparate
customer records from across the firm, apply the information consistently throughout
the site, and analyze consumer activities.

(cid:149) Real-time data collection builds intelligence. Sites must collect navigation and

interaction behavior and combine it with existing transaction, call center, and
direct marketing data. Companies should ensure that data collection persists across
all the firms(cid:146) Web sites, and that all data is stored in a central location (see the July
1998 Forrester Report (cid:147)Digital Marketing Technologies(cid:148)).

(cid:149) LDAP-compliant data sharing centralizes access. A directory server allows

companies to remember individuals across applications -- so they don(cid:146)t need to log
in or reenter information multiple times (see the June 1999 Forrester Report
(cid:147)Centralize Access Control Now(cid:148)). The server and all applications that need to
access it -- like rules management tools -- must be LDAP compliant.

(cid:149) Web activity analysis and reporting measure effectiveness. This class of tools

measures the outcomes of personalization efforts, allowing site managers to
interpret data in customer profiles. The results feed the creation of new-and-
improved business rules that reflect a deeper understanding of customer
behavior. Vendors like Andromedia and net.Genesis provide real-time analysis
and reporting. Traditional data mining tools from firms like MicroStrategy and
DataSage provide deeper offline decision support to intelligently predict product-
bundle offers such as new employee office-supply packs.

Matchmaking Engines Require Two Types Of Function
Two types of tool sets help companies match relevant content to the right individuals:

13

(cid:149) Rules management tools let marketers set policies. These tools allow firms
to manage business rules -- from tailored pricing to fraud detection. Top rules
tools -- built into BroadVision and ATG platforms or as stand-alone products like
Neuron Data -- combine easy-to-use GUI interfaces with workflow management
and consistency checking.

(cid:149) Recommendation engines learn over time. These tools use real-time data to

learn about customers(cid:146) behavior based on their actions, then make reliable
predictions about what customers are likely to buy next. These artificial
intelligence-based marketing systems rely on collaborative filtering, Baysian belief
networks, clustering algorithms, or other neural network outgrowths. Vendors
include Net Perceptions, Andromedia/LikeMinds, and RightPoint.

Sites Can Assemble A Portfolio Of Products In Three Steps
Managers need to develop a shortlist of vendors based on their assessment of gaps,
overlaps, and constraints in their existing site infrastructure (see Figure 5). To do this,
they should apply a three-step approach:

1) Grade current site capabilities. Rate existing functionality as nonexistent, basic,

or advanced. Areas that come up short require upgrades.

2) Identify solutions for weaknesses. Seek vendors to fill missing capabilities. To

reduce costs, avoid vendors with significant overlapping functionality, such as Blue
Martini and Annuncio.

3) Check for modularity. Choose tools by their ability to interoperate with other
technologies. Check for component support (COM or CORBA) consistent with
the rest of the organization, clear object model presentation, data independence,
and ability to leverage infrastructure standards like Java 2 Enterprise Edition.

Firms must move to smart personalization.
To cash in on the promise of tailored content offerings, companies need to
implement: 1) rich profiles that consolidate customer knowledge from across all
channels; 2) granular, hierarchically tagged content that leverages emerging
XML standards; and 3) matchmaking engines that target content to individuals.
Site architects will use Forrester(cid:146)s continuous commerce architecture to select
vendors that create a supporting infrastructure.

Smart personalization raises privacy stakes.
As personalization gets smart and individuals part with more personal data,
companies are facing increasing pressure to maintain consumer trust. Forrester
believes that companies face three challenges: 1) maintaining a consistent
Internet privacy policy across business channels; 2) sharing of personal profile
data with third-party partners and service providers that may have different
privacy policies, such as DoubleClick or Be Free; and 3) European data
protection standards. For protection, companies should create a (cid:147)privacy
watchdog(cid:148) position. This executive should enforce a companywide privacy
policy, ensure that third-party business partners(cid:146) policies sync up, and determine
the impact of European standards on data collection.

Place past transactional data in context.
Brick-and-mortar companies hoping to leverage their wealth of historical
customer data for the Internet must realize that this data will provide only
marginal help. Past actions do give some insight into future behavior and can
help train recommendation systems. However, online user behavior shows how
customers shop -- information far more valuable than transaction histories.
Merchants that want to avoid off-target recommendations from overeager
personalization will build user context into suggestive sells rather than taking
a shotgun approach.

Ease the forgotten user name and password headache.
Over 50% of eCommerce sites already save customer billing information for
express shopping. This overburdens consumers, who must remember all the user
names and passwords. Ease this burden -- and its associated customer service
costs -- by providing cookie options, automated password reminders, and no
restrictions on password length and format.

Firms that use customer information intelligently win.
Rich customer profiles give companies a competitive advantage in understanding
which products to pitch to whom. As sites leverage knowledge about customers,
service improves to a level that firms with less knowledge can(cid:146)t match. As a
result, the Internet will continue to reward companies like Amazon.com, which
uses its customer behavior database to draw a road map for each successive
business venture.

Traditional data mining and real-time reporting tools converge.
Companies need to better understand customers in order to more effectively
offer services. This requires real-time tools to understand a user(cid:146)s context in a
way that offline data mining tools can(cid:146)t. Look for traditional players with strong
installed bases, like MicroStrategy, to buy an expert in real-time data collection
and reporting like Andromedia.

Leaders will customize consistently across channels.
Leading firms will use unified profiles and matchmaking logic to ensure smart
interactions with customers across all touchpoints. Forrester expects that
vanguard firms will tailor experiences on ATM screens and Interactive
Voice Response (IVR) systems, let customers access wish lists and event
recommendations over the telephone, and provide matchmaking logic that call
center representatives and sales clerks can use to intelligently up- and cross-sell.

Platform battle intensifies.
BroadVision will face an onslaught of new competitors hoping to unseat the

Firms must move to smart personalization.
To cash in on the promise of tailored content offerings, companies need to
implement: 1) rich profiles that consolidate customer knowledge from across all
channels; 2) granular, hierarchically tagged content that leverages emerging
XML standards; and 3) matchmaking engines that target content to individuals.
Site architects will use Forrester(cid:146)s continuous commerce architecture to select
vendors that create a supporting infrastructure.

Smart personalization raises privacy stakes.
As personalization gets smart and individuals part with more personal data,
companies are facing increasing pressure to maintain consumer trust. Forrester
believes that companies face three challenges: 1) maintaining a consistent
Internet privacy policy across business channels; 2) sharing of personal profile
data with third-party partners and service providers that may have different
privacy policies, such as DoubleClick or Be Free; and 3) European data
protection standards. For protection, companies should create a (cid:147)privacy
watchdog(cid:148) position. This executive should enforce a companywide privacy
policy, ensure that third-party business partners(cid:146) policies sync up, and determine
the impact of European standards on data collection.

Place past transactional data in context.
Brick-and-mortar companies hoping to leverage their wealth of historical
customer data for the Internet must realize that this data will provide only
marginal help. Past actions do give some insight into future behavior and can
help train recommendation systems. However, online user behavior shows how
customers shop -- information far more valuable than transaction histories.
Merchants that want to avoid off-target recommendations from overeager
personalization will build user context into suggestive sells rather than taking
a shotgun approach.

Ease the forgotten user name and password headache.
Over 50% of eCommerce sites already save customer billing information for
express shopping. This overburdens consumers, who must remember all the user
names and passwords. Ease this burden -- and its associated customer service
costs -- by providing cookie options, automated password reminders, and no
restrictions on password length and format.
