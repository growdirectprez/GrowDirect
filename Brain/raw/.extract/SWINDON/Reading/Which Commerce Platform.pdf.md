Clashing Site Priorities Pit Feature-Richness Against Flexibility

Firms, vendors, and eCommerce integrators face a tradeoff. While features

in packaged products save site builders time, they adapt poorly to business

processes and don(cid:146)t differentiate companies from competitors. By custom

coding instead, sites can achieve competitive advantage -- but take longer

and incur the risks that come with a software development project.

COMMERCE SITE DILEMMA: TIME TO MARKET OR DIFFERENTIATION
Sites must pick a commerce platform -- a software foundation for selling on the Web.
Choices vary from feature-rich packages to open but bare-bones infrastructure. This
difficult decision challenges firms, vendors, and eCommerce integrators alike.

Site Managers Feel Conflicting Pressures
As business-to-consumer eCommerce trends to $143 billion by 2003, site managers face
intense pressures (see the September 1999 Forrester Report (cid:147)Post-Web Retail(cid:148)):

(cid:149) Results-hungry CEOs drive IT toward full-featured packages. Activist

executives expect eCommerce ROI (see the November 1998 Forrester Report
(cid:147)Getting Executive Buy-In(cid:148)). But sites that code from scratch risk delays caused
by the famous slippery schedules of software development projects. The prospect
of missing out on holiday season revenues because of a blown October deadline
drives decision-makers toward prebuilt solutions.

(cid:149) CTOs prefer open platforms that they can leverage across projects. All-in-

one packages can be quick to implement but limit IT(cid:146)s ability to customize,
integrate, and evolve a site. As a result, technology managers want products that
expose object models and use standards like Java. Otherwise, vendors(cid:146) proprietary
technologies hold eCommerce team capacity hostage.

Vendors Get Caught In The Squeeze
Just as demand for their products blossoms, established commerce platform vendors
witness threatening market shifts. Innovators that captured early customers by leading
in the feature derby now face resistance from prospects worried about site evolution.

eCommerce Integrators Cross Fingers And Pick Sides
Services companies that build sites for Global 2,500 firms have a stake in the game, too.
Asked to recommend and implement products, these firms face conflicting forces.

(cid:149) Pushing familiar products shortens schedules and fattens profits. Profits rise
when integrators(cid:146) employees fill slots on billable projects -- not seats in a vendor(cid:146)s
classroom. For this reason, after firms install a product once, they gravitate toward
it for future engagement to amortize training costs and leverage lessons learned.

(cid:149) Client needs force outsourcers to expand repertoires. eCommerce integrators
jockeying for multimillion-dollar projects respond to RFPs that emphasize specific
functional requirements, in most cases. The safest bet: Compare product features
to short-term requirements and let the closest match win.

HIGH STAKES AND FAST PACE ELIMINATE ROOM FOR ERROR
These conflicting imperatives trigger anxieties. A wrong choice can mean ripping out
millions(cid:146) worth of infrastructure for sites -- or, for suppliers, going belly up. But a wait-
and-see attitude won(cid:146)t cut it because all three camps face time-sensitive decisions.

(cid:149) Should sites tilt spending toward a software package or software engineers?

Shifting dollars from capital budgets to headcount is torturous after the fact.
When it has to happen in the third or fourth quarter, heads roll.

(cid:149) Must vendors rearchitect proprietary but successful products? New entrants
threaten established software suppliers by selling to the growing number of firms
eager to capitalize on the wave of Internet software standards.

(cid:149) Can eCommerce integrators afford to standardize on a single product?

And if so, which one? The burden of mastering new platforms is light compared
with the challenge of saving a practice built on a dead-end solution.

Firms Must Optimize For The Life -- Not The Birth -- Of The Site

Companies require a commerce platform that supports continuous

innovation. To pick the right product, commerce teams must first ensure

that they can evolve their site after launch -- initial speed to market

should come second on the priority list. But firms can have it all by

choosing products with strong app servers and tailorable applications.

COMPANIES MUST FLIP THEIR PLATFORM SELECTION PROCESS
Commerce teams need to accommodate their firms(cid:146) business processes and differentiate
from competitors. To accomplish this, companies must invert the typical vendor
selection process by screening for tailorability first, features second. Companies should:

1) Pare commerce platform contenders to products with strong app servers.

Application servers offer a platform and tool set that simplify custom-coding and
ensure that new site functions are robust and secure.

2) Pick a winner based on its tailorable features. Apps designed for tailorability

can be refined, extended, overridden, and replaced. Customizable features let sites
adapt quickly to shifting markets and competitive forces (see Figure 1).

For Swift And Reliable Evolution, Sites Require An Application Server
App servers help companies develop and run commerce sites reliably and securely with
bulletproof infrastructure for both out-of-the-box applications and custom code.

(cid:149) Productivity and quality soar for programmers. App servers(cid:146) ability to broker
interactions between components and orchestrate transactions across separate
systems lets teams integrate new features with less effort. By assembling
components, a fixed-price retailer whose competitor unveils auctions can catch
up without the slog of coding from scratch.

(cid:149) Sites gain resilience from protective infrastructure. To ensure that visitors
aren(cid:146)t kicked out by computer crashes, app servers hand off dropped sessions
to remaining live systems. They cache data and pool scarce resources like database
connections to fend off overload, and offer tools to monitor systems(cid:146) health.

(cid:149) Firms protect investments with a consistent foundation and standards. Once

To Fast-Track Launch But Avoid Dead Ends, Sites Need Tailorable Apps
A commerce platform(cid:146)s applications must not only deliver basic features like catalogs,
payment, tax calculation, checkout, and order status, but also allow site customization.

(cid:149) Sites jump-start commerce with features designed to be changed. Tailorable

apps publish object models and APIs that stick to an underlying app server(cid:146)s
standards. They offer tools to configure critical data like merchandising rules and
catalog content. This lets sites evolve without waiting for a vendor(cid:146)s next release.

(cid:149) Firms differentiate and evolve sites by replacing apps. To let firms make

radical site changes, tailorable apps offer frameworks to substitute or add features.
This lets sites make major advances like replacing a fixed-price static catalog with
a syndicated multisupplier version that delivers demand-based pricing.

characteristics, and the amount of interest expressed by our clients. This narrowed the

environment, we gave more weight to products(cid:146) application servers and to the
tailorability of their applications (see Figure 2).

Turmoil Ahead For Market Veterans
Five early entrants with significant customer bases share a need to retool products
to a nonproprietary app server. This foretells a rocky road for customers as:

11

(cid:149) Open Market and Sun/Netscape race to transform. Both vendors are in

transition as they shed proprietary infrastructure in favor of Netscape Application
Server (NAS). Open Market jump-started its effort by acquiring NAS-based
FutureTense but has a long way to go (see Figure 9). Sun/Netscape(cid:146)s challenge,
meanwhile, is to migrate its Xpert suite to NAS and morph NAS into the iPlanet
J2EE app server (see Figure 10). While these vendors transform, firms that

2000, both will resurface as strong platforms.

(cid:149) InterWorld and BroadVision take half-hearted steps. InterWorld delivers the

largest set of tailorable commerce apps, supported by first-class configuration tools

excels at personalization (see Figure 12). But both lack plans to move away from
their proprietary app servers. Instead of incremental steps to interoperate with
Enterprise Java Beans (EJB) or COM, these vendors should take the plunge and
switch to a standards-based app server from an infrastructure heavy like BEA(cid:146)s
WebLogic, or an embeddable product like Progress Software(cid:146)s Apptivity.

(cid:149) Vignette mistakes its content management for full commerce. A limited,

proprietary app server as well as the lack of basic features like order processing
and a shopping cart make StoryServer a poor choice as a foundation for selling
sites. Vignette(cid:146)s strong suit remains content management. The vendor recently
announced that it will partner with IBM to offer a powerful combination --
StoryServer and Net.Commerce on WebSphere (see Figure 13).

How to select a commerce platform.
To pick a commerce platform, firms should identify products with strong app
servers, then pick a winner based on application tailorability.

Platform selection teams must include a software architect -- a pro with the
advanced skills required to design on component models, derive class
hierarchies, and compose object interfaces. These gurus will save team members(cid:146)
jobs by steering clear of dead-end products. Don(cid:146)t have a real architect on staff?
Rent one -- not from integrators biased by vendor partnerships. Opt for a brain
shop like Eliassen Group instead.

Firms not shackled to Microsoft must adopt the Java platform.
Only Microsoft still sees Java as (cid:147)just a language.(cid:148) All the products Forrester
examined for this report except Site Server Commerce Edition are moving
toward the Java platform in some measure. Meanwhile, integrators are too busy
building real Java platform-based, mission-critical systems to bother arguing
against Redmond(cid:146)s party line. Forrester believes Java is now a wise strategic
platform choice for enterprise-strength reliability and integration. It(cid:146)s also the
right path for firms eager to attract the best technical talent.

Most commerce platform vendors shouldn(cid:146)t build app servers.
Few of today(cid:146)s commerce platform vendors have what it takes to keep playing
both sides of the court -- application server and commerce apps. Of the
contenders examined in this report, only IBM and Microsoft possess the
necessary enterprise clout, deep pockets, and ecosystems of partners. Other
vendors must sever their commerce apps from proprietary infrastructure and
deliver products built on a strong third-party app server.

Integrators must retire practices built on proprietary products.
Service provider firms should recenter their portfolios on commerce platforms
with standards-based app servers like ATG Dynamo. Investing in skills required
to build sites on open commerce platforms lessens risk because the skills apply
across projects and products. Cambridge Technology Partners, for example,
should phase out its BroadVision practice and keep only maintenance resources
required to service its One-To-One-based sites, until -- or unless -- BroadVision
sees the light and decides to migrate away from its proprietary app server.

W H A T   I T   M E A N S

The commerce components market blossoms.
Application servers(cid:146) new importance, combined with Java 2 platform
standardization, will lead to a strong market for third-party commerce
components. This will put component suppliers like EC Cubed in the limelight
and create renewed interest in online component marketplaces like Flashline --
a site that resells JavaBeans and ActiveX components.

Some commerce applications become networked services.
Among plug-in commerce application components, some rely on complex data
from external sources like tax calculation, shipping, or logistics. Why keep them
local? These functions will transition from software installed at commerce sites
to networked services. TAXWARE(cid:146)s product, for example, will no longer be
embedded in commerce platforms and require periodic updates. The tax
software vendor will simply install a few instances of its service on main Internet
backbones -- and meter the service for sites that use it.

Midtier app server vendors find an escape hatch.
App server vendors like SilverStream and Bluestone will follow the lead of ATG
and Allaire and offer commerce apps on top of their products. This new
beachhead will also be their escape route from building infrastructure products
when the app server market shakes down to a few heavyweights like BEA,
Oracle, and IBM. The exception: Forrester believes Allaire will continue to excel
at the object model layer between the app server and commerce applications --
but port it to a J2EE base.

Integrators(cid:146) new assets: vertical components.
Commerce platforms based on nonproprietary app servers with tailorable
applications will become the norm. Integrators will seize this opportunity
to capitalize their principal asset: understanding. Consulting firms that until
now simply documented best practices and lessons learned through knowledge
repositories will build reusable commerce component repositories. This will
lessen the blow of employee churn while increasing integrators(cid:146) profitability
through a more highly leveraged business model.

Will Persistence persist? If not, J2EE life insurance comes to the rescue.
