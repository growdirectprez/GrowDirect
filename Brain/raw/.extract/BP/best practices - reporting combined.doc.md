Financial Reporting Policy/Process
Best Practice Option
Best practices in financial reporting centers on paring down management reports, streamlining the process, and increasing the usefulness of reports for non-financial managers.  Analysis reveals the following best practices for reporting:

Paring-down Management Reports
Standard report format with fewer rather than more measures
Report variances on an exception basis
De-emphasize historical reporting

Streamlining Process
Allow flexible overhead allocations
De-emphasize data collection/manipulation
Eliminate excess reporting and paper
Reduce reporting cycle time by actively reducing the number of reports generated
Continuously improve reporting deadlines
Provide activity based management (ABM) or activity based costing (ABC) through accrual accounting, sophisticated costing systems, and more effective reports
Embed report writing expertise in the accounting staff, to eliminate reliance on MIS
Procedures documented to ensure conformity to GAAP
Regulatory and external reporting requirements identified and documented

Increasing Usefulness
Emphasize change and trend information, rather than absolutes
Include profitability analysis by product, market, geography, and customer
Provide a comparison against industry and/or competitors by product/service
Use a balanced set of measures (P&L, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow)
Align report with business and user requirements
Perform cause and effect analysis between cost and operational data
Incorporate and link financial and non-financial data in alignment with business objectives and key results
Encourage changing management reports each year to reflect new priorities
Assess customer satisfaction with financial reports through cross-functional team efforts
Link financial forecasts to supply/demand planning and pricing activities
Use business reasons, not accounting explanations for variances identified

Examples of organizations who have utilized best practices for reporting:

Kmart, Target, and Wal-Mart are in the process of implementing activity based costing (ABC), or identifying all costs associated with selling a product.  Kmart is in the research and development phase, Wal-Mart is likely to roll out the program in 1998 or early 1999, and Target is currently using ABC in conjunction with vendors.

An international shipping company restructured their reporting so that management received the right information to make business decisions, focusing on concise, relevant, forward looking, and easily understood information.  This move especially helped operations in government reporting requirements.

A manufacturer of automobile parts restructured reporting formats and methods to provide information on individual activity basis.  Both finance and operational managers contributed to the formulation of the report.  The effort resulted in management being able to identify and focus on value added activities.   Management now views employees not as mere resources to run equipment but as resources capable of generating new ideas.

An international sports company moved away from actual/budget to trend data reporting, incorporated non-financial information in their reports, and consolidated much of their financial information.  This resulted in more timely reporting that is more helpful to business managers. 

 An Australian government agency is implementing ABM in order to optimize resource allocation, evaluate the cost/benefit of strategic options, distinguish between controllable and uncontrollable costs and the magnitude of each, and encourage managers to exercise more control over their costs.

A large soft drink manufacturer is using ABM to develop business strategies by analyzing customer profitability and segments.  This information has led to improved business profitability by identifying non-value added costs and the high value adding cost as they relate to customer service.

Several utilities in the U.K. have implemented ABM to evaluate their customer base.

A large European steel company standardized their costing (using ABM) and established common cost centers to formulate budgets within the same cost structures.  This resulted in improved pricing methods, sales accountability, and control (which led to a decrease in the difference between actuals and budget.)

An international paper manufacturer in the U.S. redesigned their financial measuring, reporting, and accounting systems.  As a result of the reporting changes, production supervisors and management find that they are able to understand the financial information presented.  The data, including throughput, operating expenses, inventory, and key non-financial measures, is presented to summarize commitment and make common sense.  Accounting no longer sees itself as the financial police department, but as a business partner that works side by side with the production department.

An U.S. automobile manufacturer implemented ABM to improve efficiency, cut costs, boost quality, and rejuvenate its product line.  The implementation generated hundreds of millions of dollars in benefits by helping simplify product designs and eliminate unproductive, inefficient, or redundant activities.  It was found that the benefits were 10-20 times greater than the investment in the program, and as some sites, 50-100 times the implementation cost.

Financial Reporting Technology
Best Practice Option
Best practices in financial reporting and planning focus on improving the accuracy and timeliness of reports, decreasing manual interaction, and standardizing the format and process.  Analysis reveals the following best practices for reporting and planning:

Improving Accuracy and Timeliness
Develop flash reports after the first day of close
Deliver reports in electronic format automatically to users
Emphasize no re-keying of data
Develop an Electronic Information System (EIS) that replaces the need for report preparation

Increasing Usefulness
Reports available on-line and ad hoc reporting requests can be easily accommodated
Provide on-line access to information with drill down capability
Allows on-line feedback from users
Use effective and flexible report writing to minimize manipulation on PCs
System generated standard reports allow manipulation of data to incorporate graphics
Capability in the general ledger to highlight variances over 20%

Standardizing Format and Process
Implement a single data warehouse for all financial and non-financial data
Link the general ledger to a financial modeling system that supports two way communications for ease of reporting
Download to PC based packages for consolidation/analysis for review of variances/exceptions
Automate both  regulatory and external reports as much as possible
External reports are automatically generated
Use of G/L as intended, as reporting tool, not data warehouse

Key performance indicators and benchmarks include the following:

Performance Measures/Benchmarks		Best Practice

Percentage of time spent on reviews and presentations                                  	Less than 10%
Cycle time for senior management to get reports	                                                         3 Days

Examples of organizations who have utilized best practices for reporting:

A U.S. banking group with 140 branches adopted a integrated reporting and consolidation system that allowed them to produce reports more frequently, faster, and in more detail than previously.  The number of days to produce consolidated reports decreased from 10 to 4 days and an intelligent and flexible exception reporting enhances reporting and the ability of managers to run their operations.

A beverage company implemented a data warehouse that resulted in tighter controls, a new information culture, and a faster/more effective way to create reports and implement changes.

A large decentralized computer company realized it used 315 systems to support 6 accounting functions, including general ledger, fixed assets, accounts receivable, accounts payable, U.S. transactions, and international transactions.  As a result, the company could not respond quickly to changing information requirements, could not consolidate financial information efficiently, and found the redundancy in developing and maintaining the systems expensive.

A large U.S. financial services organization automated over 1,000 regulatory reports, which resulted in a reduction in computer mainframe costs by 20% and a reduction in finance staff from 500 to 200.
