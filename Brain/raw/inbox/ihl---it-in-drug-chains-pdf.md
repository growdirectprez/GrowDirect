i. Introduction
i.1 Introduction / Background and Objectives
In this report, IHL Consulting Group reviews the current Drug Store chain segment of the
North American Retail market (defined as US and Canada for this report). We will look at
recent history, current situations, and future issues and examine some specific challenges
faced by those in the Drug Store segment. Drug Store chain departments are reviewed in our
“How a Drug Store works” section, with a special eye towards IT systems involved in each.
Finally, we look at a profile of each of the top US Drug Store chains; their competitive
position, their financials, their hardware and software systems, and their key personnel.
We will give the reader a working knowledge of the industry; how chains operate, what they
try to do, and what they try to avoid. Pertinent aspects of IT will be explored. We are
confident that the reader will come away from this report with a more rounded understanding
of how technology plays an increasingly vital part in the progress of the Drug Store chain
industry.
Drug Stores trace their roots through the practice of pharmacy to ancient times. Pharmacy,
historically linked to the Greek word pharmakon (meaning “remedy”), began as a set of
practices in a variety of global cultures. Whether called priest, shaman or medicine man,
ancients explored medicinal and religious purposes by mixing various plant materials into
medicinal cocktails. Specialization emerged in the 9th century around Baghdad as the
herbalist’s duties were separated from the doctor’s. Quickly spreading to Europe, this
separation was standardized (focused on compounding and dispensing medication) and the
science took on a more non-religious flavor. A recognized branch of science by the Modern
era, pharmacology took clear shape in the western Industrial Revolution. Pharmacy schools
began opening in the mid-1800’s as the practice became more professionalized and
regulated. Around the time of World War II, the role of the pharmacist began to change as the
pharmaceutical industry took off and took over the process of the discovery and
compounding of drugs.
At the same time, the “business” of the pharmacy was changing. A successful young
pharmacist, Charles R. Walgreen, purchased his first community pharmacy store in Chicago
in 1902. The Walgreen Company was incorporated in 1909 and began its expansion. By the
time of his death in 1939, Walgreen’s had grown to 490 stores. Taking some retail cues from
the historic corner store (quickly turning into the modern grocery store), Walgreen pioneered
innovations like open-display merchandising and the ever-popular lunch counter. The
traditional community pharmacy evolved into the local Drug Store. And as urban populations
moved to the suburbs, the local Drug Store fast became a member of the new, shiny
shopping center.
As the industry grew, and the American doctor used more and better drugs in his everyday
practice, Drug Stores expanded and consolidated. The industry became a big business in its
own right, as entrepreneurial local and regional groups consolidated into larger and larger
national chains. This trend of mergers and acquisitions accelerated in the 90’s. The
prescription market accelerated as well. Chains began to see that they didn’t just have to
compete with each other, but with other categories of business from the convenience store to
the supermarket. Size, economy of scale and improved marketing are the tactics Drug Stores

i.2 Drug Store Chains Defined
For the purposes of our analysis, we are defining a Drug Store as any self-service retail store
selling a full line of prescription drugs and patent medicines, as well as a number of related
products such as cosmetics, toiletries, household items, convenience and seasonal
merchandise (called “front-end” products). This includes those establishments historically
known as pharmacies. For the purpose of this report, “chain” refers to stores who are a part
of a network of at least three or more commonly owned establishments. This does not
include stores that may carry the above-mentioned goods as part of a wider assortment of
merchandise (mass merchandisers such as Wal-Mart and Kmart, warehouse clubs such as
Sam’s Club and Costco or supermarkets). Nor does it include those establishments
commonly referred to as convenience stores; compact, drive-to stores offering a limited line
of high-convenience goods such as those listed above. Please refer to our other retail
segment reports on these industries.
