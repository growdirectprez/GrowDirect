Enthusiasm Propels Personalization

eCommerce managers expect personalization to make their sites easier

to use and to boost sales. Most interviewees have a gut feeling that their

efforts are succeeding, even though few measure results. As they deploy a

variety of solutions, managers struggle to craft rules that determine what

content to deliver and how to make the technology work.

EXPERIMENTS WITH PERSONALIZATION
Forrester last year asked 50 site managers why their companies had made so little
progress with personalization (see the April 1998 Forrester Report (cid:147)Pragmatic
Personalization(cid:148)). We found that scattered customer information, unstructured content,
and a lack of business analysis were holding merchants back. This month, Forrester
checked the progress of personalization on the Web by interviewing 21 business-to-
business and 23 business-to-consumer merchants that customize content for individuals.

Interviewees Expect Many Benefits From Tailored Content
The online merchants we interviewed want personalization to accomplish goals that
range from making their sites easier to use to increasing sales (see Figure 1-1).
Expectations and enthusiasm run high.

(cid:147)People want specifics, and what they want changes drastically from individual to
individual. Our goal with personalization is to satisfy a user(cid:146)s needs. This allows us
to build a competitive advantage, create switching barriers, and just be better than
everyone else.(cid:148) (Publishing and printing company)

(cid:147)The end goal is to increase repeat business. We believe that putting more
information under a consumer(cid:146)s control will lead to more attractive features and
that we(cid:146)ll see increased revenue and traffic as he comes back for those features.(cid:148)
(Retail company)

Most Guess That They Are Producing Results
While only 16% of respondents measure the impact of their efforts, most say that they
have a gut feeling that personalization is worth the investment.

Interviewees Try Many Forms Of Personalization
Our respondents customize their sites in diverse ways. While 64% use opt-in email
to send tailored content or promotional messages, interviewees also implement wish
lists, customized pricing, tailored marketing messages, and configurable home pages
(see Figure 1-2).

(cid:147)Personalization means a lot of different things to us: It is opt-in email messaging,
the product shown to the user when she logs in, her account history, and the
ability to always change or adjust her account information.(cid:148) (Retail company)

(cid:147)For us, personalization is the ability for users to choose which information will
appear on a page and to package their own offering. People want control over the
subject matter: We can(cid:146)t guess all the ways they(cid:146)ll use our data, so we let them
tailor the site to their interests. We can watch that and learn which information is
most relevant.(cid:148) (Publishing and printing company)

Merchants Use Myriad Technologies To Tailor Sites
The 41% of interviewees who use software from vendors like BroadVision and Net
Perceptions laud the prepackaged functionality and easy-to-configure business rules of
those options. In contrast, those who built their personalization applications in-house
say that they hit walls when they try to add more sophisticated features like
merchandising rules and automated recommendations.

(cid:147)We built personalization ourselves around an Oracle database. Today, I might buy
something like BroadVision instead. BroadVision is incredible in terms of how
you can configure the software. However, we haven(cid:146)t switched over because the
upfront configuration process would probably be equal to the effort of building it
ourselves.(cid:148) (Electronics company)

(cid:147)We refer to the technology here as the old Model T of Web development. We use
Unix servers, a Netscape Web server, an Oracle database, and applications written
in Perl. We chose to do it that way because it was the lowest cost at the time.
We absolutely plan to move to a packaged solution.(cid:148) (Auto manufacturer)

Interview Conclusions
From our conversations with executives in charge of their site(cid:146)s personalization efforts,
we conclude that:

(cid:149) Companies continue to experiment. Interviewees try many types of

personalized features in attempts to build relationships and increase sales.

(cid:149) Managers feel optimistic about personalization(cid:146)s future. While they have little
evidence that customizing content provides any return on investment, executives
say that they believe that it is worth the effort.

(cid:149) Delivering on the promise proves difficult. Developing effective rules and

getting technology to work surface as top concerns.

A N A L Y S I S

Sites Must Step Up To Smart Personalization

Although our interviewees are bullish on their personalization efforts,

most lack the clear goals, supporting infrastructure, and robust technology

to deliver value to site visitors. As a result, their customers receive a

disappointing payoff. To realize personalization(cid:146)s potential, sites must

build an architecture that recognizes individuals, and then customizes

content, sales, and service to their needs. Merchants must integrate

best-of-breed applications to accomplish these goals.

REALITY CONTRADICTS OPTIMISM
Interviewee enthusiasm about customized offerings clashes with reality as
personalization efforts fail to overcome today(cid:146)s unimpressive 2% look-to-buy ratios.
In fact, retailer optimism fades over time -- inexperienced site managers think that
personalization will solve their customer retention problems, but experienced managers
believe the answer lies in better service (see the June 1999 Forrester Report (cid:147)Making
Net Shoppers Loyal(cid:148)). The reason is that today(cid:146)s sites fail either to understand
customers or to actively perform services for them -- or both (see Figure 2). This
misguided personalization wastes customers(cid:146) time and annoys them as:

(cid:149) Lazy personalizers don(cid:146)t leverage their data. Knowing a customer(cid:146)s allergies
should help an online drugstore to screen out pain relievers that might trigger a
reaction. Unfortunately, even after we entered aspirin into an allergy profile at
PlanetRx, the site promoted -- and let us buy -- Aleve, a product that warns away
those with allergies to aspirin.

(cid:149) Trivial personalizers underwhelm. The New York Life, General Motors, and
Fleet Bank sites lure visitors into volunteering their preferences and personal
information in return for tailored content. But instead of a customized insurance
profile, personal auto maintenance suggestions, or specific financial advice, these
companies insult customers with (cid:147)tailored(cid:148) menus of marketing messages.

(cid:149) Overeager personalizers make faulty assumptions. Even the big guns can
stumble by leaping to a bad conclusion based on inadequate information --
Outpost.com cuts its margins by recommending the $19.95 surge protector after
customers have already put the $59.95 version in their shopping carts.

Companies Lose Revenues
The result of these misguided efforts is that sites leave money on the table. Ineffective
personalizers reap three painful results:

(cid:149) Poor customer retention. When personalization misfires, customers never

return to view their uncompelling customized content. Their next trip back to the
site comes when they show up to turn off the annoying opt-in email that never
delivered value but did clog up their mailboxes with daily generic offers.

(cid:149) Lower margins. Unable to satisfy customer needs with value-added services,
companies like Sears and Service Merchandise must lower prices to compete
against comparison engines, auctions, and aggregators.

(cid:149) Lost sales. Most companies only convert 2% of lookers to buyers -- or less.

Despite energies spent on simplifying navigation, speeding checkout, reducing
first-time buying risk, and enhancing loyalty with incentives, many customers still
just kick the tires and leave.
