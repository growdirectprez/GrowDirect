Organizational Structure – Centralized/Matrixed and Shared Services:  General Accounting, Accounts Payable, Accounts Receivable, Payroll

Best Practice Option

World-class organizations organize their finance functions centrally with a matrixed functionality where high volume, transaction based processes are clustered together in a shared service structure.  Best practices delineates the following regarding finance functions that are centralized and employ shared services:

Definition of Shared Services:

Shared Services can be defined as:
 “Any processes within an enterprise that have a separate set of dedicated resources (e.g. people or assets) managed through a single organization that serve more than one business unit.”
Centralized/matrixed and shared service organizations are designed to provide lower costs and one-stop services to all parts of the corporation.  Under centralized/matrixed and shared services, scattered operations such as finance or human resources are pulled together into a mega-service center, serving all parts of a company’s business units around the globe
Shared services centralizes support staff
Routine, high volume transactions and specialized skills can be performed by specific geographic centers to support multiple business units
Centralized/matrixed and shared services processes and procedures are standardized
Processes for centralized shared services include:
General accounting such as general ledger, fixed assets, intercompany accounting, and financial reporting
Purchasing such as automated purchasing, common items/commodities, vendor master/quality assurance
Disbursements such as accounts payable, travel and entertainment, check requests
Revenues such as order entry, revenue transactions, sales administration
Treasury management such as cash management and foreign exchange
Employee services such as payroll, benefits, employee records, training, recruiting
Information technology such as data center, help desk and programming
Internal audit
Financial/Management reporting
Research and development
Facilities management
Corporate communication
Legal services


Shared Services vs. Just Corporate Centralization:

The following characteristics distinguish shared services from only corporate centralization. Shared services are:
Jointly created service level agreements between the shared service center and the business unit supported
A service provider to a diverse set of business units
Accountable for quality and responsiveness
Focused on their internal customer
Located based on business needs, cost of operations and employee demographics
Have a business strategy to enhance value to company
Establish contracts with business units with no guarantee of renewal
Business units participate in shared services governance

Corporate centralization:
Mandated policies and timeliness
A required company function
Accountable for deadlines and costs
Corporate officer focus
Located at corporate headquarters
Cost reduction strategy
No options, no contracts
Business units do not participate in governance

Implementation:
Starting points for shared services include: 1) consolidation of the organization by taking out layers of management; 2) process and systems standardization; and 3) reengineering processes
One of the most important factors for success is implementing common and integrated systems
Best practices shows that organizations have successfully restructured decentralized operational groups to form shared service organizations

Proven Savings:
Companies have typically recouped implementation costs in approximately 2-3 years
According to research conducted, sharing services traditional duplicated in different operating units of an organization can reduce costs in some areas of finance and accounting by 50% to 75%
Shared services provide cost savings and achieve common systems or standards
A study of European finance activities at 20 major multinational companies found they can save an average of $10 million or 35-45% of finance function costs by sharing mechanical accounting functions on a regional or pan-European basis, rather than country by country



Organizational Examples:

Wal-Mart
Wal-Mart and Wal-Mart International have joined at the senior management level and several functional levels, including technology, finance, and logistics.  The day-to-day operations, however, remain separate.

Dayton-Hudson
Dayton-Hudson Corporation has been combining certain functions across their many distinct businesses, including Target, Mervyns, and Department Stores (Dayton’s, Hudson’s, and Marshall Field’s stores).  These functions include finance, executives, information systems (integrated in 1997), credit card operations, and human resources.  Ultimately, they plan to intertwine all divisions in order to leverage common resources and create a “boundaryless” organization.  Dayton-Hudson sees this transition to a “best practice structure” as a unique strength of Dayton Hudson, as it “drives knowledge transfer between the divisions, economies of scale, and a coordinated effort for product development and production.”

Sears, Roebuck & Co.
Sears has implemented a shared service center that integrates human resources, payroll, and systems.  While reviewing and improving processes, Sears determined that this structure better meets the needs of the corporation.  (See Best Practice Option – Payroll Structure for the benefits of this service.)  Sears asserts that the implementation of a shared service center (where the operational divisions were free to contract outside services if internal performance failed to meet expectations) was critical to the accrual of benefits from the restructuring.

Alcoa
Implemented a unique T&E system in finance that eliminated repetitive steps and slashed processing costs; the new system helped to establish a shared services organization strategically within the company
Reduced error rates substantially; saved 30 minutes of traveler time per report; increased the availability of information horizontally across the company; saves $200,000 annually and approximately $2 million in “soft” savings

Allied Signal
Consolidated 17 data centers into a single data processing mega-center
Achieved savings of approximately $42 million

Baxter Healthcare
Set up a centralized shared service center in 1988 that now processes nine million invoices per year for 10 different businesses in the US and Canada
Cost of invoice processing in 1994 is $.35 per invoice as compared to $.64 per invoice in 1988

Digital Equipment
Overall corporate goal to improve productivity, quality, and flexibility by reducing duplication, eliminating redundancies, and simplifying accounting processes was achieved through shared services
$12 to $15 million of hard dollar savings have been documented; more than 150 duplicate systems have been eliminated; hard copy reporting is no longer required
In Europe, consolidated 17 service centers into three that operate as a single network and provide a single “central warehouse” of information
Expecting 50% savings within two years

General Electric
Established a financial services organization that consolidated accounting work in 50 decentralized locations into four large centers
Achieved economies of scale and facilitated the installation of common systems
Consolidation activities lowered employment by 30%; reduced the number of accounting systems by nearly 80%; doubled the span of management control from 7:1 to 15:1

ITT Corporation
Combined dozens of small, scattered telecommunications groups into a single unit that services the entire corporation

Johnson and Johnson
Moved to a shared services organization when the company decided to improve the effectiveness and efficiencies of the finance organization
Formed a committee called the Common  Financial Systems Steering Committee to plan, evaluate, and recommend solutions; included select CFOs who were key process owners
Improvement teams have made steady improvements such as corporate payroll streamlining overtime policies from 57 to 14

McGraw-Hill
Reorganized finance into a central unit and created teams to address each critical area
Significantly reduced costs; signaled the company’s new strategy for corporate-wide change renewed focus on the customer

Pfizer, Inc.
Converted data processing, telecommunications and PC-LAN management to shared services
Achieved savings of $3-$4 million

Rhone-Poulenc Inc
Consolidated divisions and staff and moved services such as  human resources; health, safety, and environment; and communications into a shared services organization as part of a major reengineering effort
Newly-formed business units retained functions that have to do with directly serving the customer but are too small to sustain corporate functions on their own; these are now obtained from the shared services organization

Structure of Shared Services:  General Accounting, Accounts Payable, Accounts Receivable, Payroll

Best Practice Option

There are three best practice variations of centralized and matrixed shared services structures.  Best practices indicate that optimal arrangements depend on the circumstances of the enterprise.  Which ever model is employed, the key is to strike a balance between “service” and “control”.  The three key models consist of the following:

Centralized global center – where shared service center is in one central location and services the entire worldwide business
Centralized around business structure – where shared service center may have multiple locations in strategically placed in geographic areas where the business concentrates its presence
Centralized around “Centers of Excellence” – where the shared service center is centralized, but structured according to type of service offered, i.e., shared service center would have payroll center of excellence, AP center of excellence, etc.

Best practice reporting structures include an oversight board to direct operations and assist in conflict resolution.  40% of best practice shared service centers have an oversight board.  The oversight board tends to be comprised of 5-8 key customer groups (to ensure customer representation) and includes the director of support services.
