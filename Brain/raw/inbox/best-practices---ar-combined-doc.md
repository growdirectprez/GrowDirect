Accounts Receivable (A/R) process
Best Practice Option
Best practices for accounts receivable centers on improved tracking of customer accounts and management of customer relationship.  Analysis reveals the following best practices for accounts receivable process:

Policy/Process

Use credit teams to manage customer relationship
Use of quantitative goals for A/R performance
Multifunctional teams to resolve deduction issues
Develop arrangements with customers to send fewer invoices
Effective customer reporting and tracking to differentiate between good and consistently delinquent debtors

Key performance indicators and benchmarks include the following:

Performance Measures/Benchmarks		Best Practice
Accuracy of cash application to receivables	                             97%
Average personnel cost per line item billed                                                          	$0.85
Average days sales outstanding                                                                             	28
Percent of invoices paid on time (by customer)                                                          	94%
Total cost of A/R / $1000 revenues                                                                      	$0.28

Examples of organizations that have utilized best practices include:

A major credit card company sent out 160 million statements to customers in 1990.  The accuracy rate for statements is in excess of 99.9%, which reduces the costs associated with dealing with complaints and write–offs. In addition they reduced the number of days it takes to send the bills out from the cut–off date for charges from three to five days to a day and a half. This has a direct impact upon cash flow and interest costs. Overall the cost of mailing a bill has declined by 25% over a two-year period.

Accounts Receivable (A/R) Technology
Best Practice Option
Best practices for A/R focus on automating collection and notification and integrating A/R with other financial and operational systems.  Analysis reveals the following best practices for accounts receivable technology:

Integration with other functions
Integration of order entry, billing, accounts receivable and general ledger systems
Electronic signature capture at register

Automation
Electronic payment and automated matching to receivables
Automatic match of credit notes and invoices
Automatic write-off of underpayments within accepted tolerances
On-line access to customer history/balances
On-line processing of debit and credit memos that are automatically generated for mailing to customers
Electronic transfer of bank remittance data to accounts receivable system
Auto-generation of invoice from delivery notes (with the use of EDI)
Automatic notification of accounts that are more than 35 days past due
Auto-generation of reminders for overdue accounts
Auto-notification for late payments
Automated telephone follow-up for collections on returned checks
Electronic matching/posting of remittances
Automatic account merges
Eliminate paper invoices through use of electronic billing

Key performance indicators and benchmarks include the following:

Performance Measures/Benchmarks		Best Practice
Electronic matching/posting of remittances                                                                    80%
Cost of sales and A/R Departments as a % of sales                                                       0.172%
Percent of billings that are error-free                                                                   	99%
Percent of payments that are first time matches                                                 	98%
Cycle time to process (working days)                                                                   	5 days
Number of dishonored checks per billion dollars in sales                                        	18,960
Dishonored checks processed per clerk per month                                                        1,722


Examples of organizations that have utilized best practices include:

Bank of America and Hypercom have combined in a study program to provide electronic signature capture for small and mid-sized retailers.  Electronic signature is viewed as an additional weapon against disputed purchases that are charged back to the merchant or retailer.  In addition, Sears Roebuck and Co implemented electronic signature at 800 stores and believes the system will eliminate paper storage.  Sears expects to eliminate 2,500 miles worth of receipt paper per year and the associated storage and retrieval costs.

A large multinational petroleum company took steps to re-engineer their A/R (located in treasury) to improve efficiency.  Paper checks were converted into EFT to speed the cash cycle.  Automated lockbox reporting was used, where the bank reports incoming payment details which was matched with entries in the accounts receivable system.  Bank ratings are updated on a timely basis.  This was achieved by the use of an electronic bulletin board.  In addition, the electronic reporting capability of a rating service was utilized.

A large company was able to re-engineer their A/R by integrating EDI/EFT into the process.  As a result, the processing time decreased from 2-4 days to 8 hours, overall A/R costs decreased, and less mistakes and communications errors were made.

Functional Area/ Process Impacted

Private Label Credit Card
Best Practice Option
There are three basic options for retailers that have credit cards:
	Private Label In-House
	Private Label Outsource
	Co-Branded Cards

Most retailers either outsource their operations or use co-branded credit cards.  Those companies that choose to keep their credit card operations in-house are looking for more control over their customer contact.  They are also concerned about extending credit to a larger number of customers, then an outsourced or co-branded organization may allow.  Private label in-house credit card retailers tend to be more generous with their application criteria. This will cause some problems with higher loan losses for uncollected accounts.  The retailer may choose to mitigate this risk, by offering a higher interest rate (typically around 20%).  It is not surprising to find in-house private label credit cards operations that are showing a loss, however, they may be generating significant revenue and encouraging repeat purchases.

Retailers choose to outsource their private label credit card operations because they lack the capability or skill set to manage it in-house, the volume is not large enough to support an in-house operation or because they would like to get receivables of assets off their balance sheets.  They have to carry additional assets to cover accounts, they are funding these assets at high interest rates and will in-turn raise their equity ratio, and depress their ROE.  Therefore retailers may choose to outsource their credit card operations to raise their ROE.  Outsourcing is may also be more cost efficient for the retailer.  Most private label credit cards tend to carry low balances ($300) but many accounts, whereas Visa/Mastercard credit cards carry much higher balances ($2000) with the same amount of accounts.  This means that the operational cost per account is much higher for the private label credit card.  In addition by outsourcing the credit card operations the retailer does not assume the majority of the risk.  GE Capital is the company that most retailers use for outsourcing.  They charge the retailer per statement or per account on file.  They actually manage the credit card portfolio, and will take the loan loss up to a certain point, at which time the retailer is charged for the loan losses.

Other retailers choose to use co-branded credit cards.  Co-branded cards are typically Visa or Mastercard credit cards with the retailer’s name on them.  This credit card can be used not only in the retailer’s store but in other stores as well, it will act the same way as a bank issued Visa/Mastercard credit card.  Retailers will choose to use co-branded card for the same reasons they may choose to outsource; they lack the capability to manage their own credit card operation, the volume is not large enough to support a credit card operation, they are looking for cost efficiencies or the retailer would like to raise their return on equity.  In this situation the credit card issuer takes all the risks and responsibilities of the operations.  The retailer would however have access to their customers spending patterns across retailers, including competitive spending.
