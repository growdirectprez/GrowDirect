Best Practice Option

Structure
Centralize A/P organization with a multi-functional staff
Co-locate of A/P and Purchasing
Structure teams around commodities
Centrally schedule and perform payment

Accounts Payable (A/P) Alliances with Vendors

Best Practice Option
Best practices for A/P alliances with vendors focuses on creating a strategic role for finance and accounting with vendor purchase orders, management, and relationships.  Analysis reveals the following best practices:

Strategic Role with Purchase Orders
Involve accounts payable representatives in supplier negotiations, to specify critical parameters for the settlement process
Set firm quantitative goals for number of vendors, by commodity

Strategic Management of Vendors
Pursue vendor qualification and certification; involve vendors in self-administering these programs
Capture information on vendor performance in automated vendor information system and review regularly
Capture vendor usage information on a company-wide basis for use in negotiating favorable rates and volume discounts
Outsource all freight payments, to enable focus on more high-value transactions

Strategic Relationship with Vendors
Reduce the number of POs and invoices by having vendors combine invoice orders
Reduce vendor base using commodity teams
Focus on a small set of vendors and treat them as strategic business partners
Operate in a partnership with key vendors for technical advice
Establish partnerships with key vendors built around long-term contracts

Examples of organizations who have utilized best practices for accounts payable:

A large manufacturer, with purchases in excess of $5 billion, commonly recognized as having best practices in procurement, works closely with suppliers.  They believe that to increase quality and decrease cycle time and costs, this close involvement is essential.  This company has 780 suppliers with 590 being approved (this number has decreased recently), 140 as key or preferred, 40 as certified, and 10 as strategic alliances.   The “lion’s share”, or 80%, of purchases are from 349 suppliers.  The company believes this to have been critical in cutting their workforce by half in order to remain competitive.

A large U.S. electronics manufacturer has 75,000 terminals linked to purchasing systems worldwide. The system is linked to suppliers through provided software. Through this software, orders can be processed via EDI; confirmations can be made; and suppliers can gain access to forecasts and inventory.  Highlights and results include:
The system contains a “Smart” system to check the accuracy of orders.
Worldwide contracts are developed and then logged into the worldwide agreements database.
An electronic, paperless requisitions process is used for over 4,000 authorized personnel (others can gain electronic approval when necessary).
Carries out “buyerless buying” on 60% of its requisitions — buyers intervene only for exceptions.
Benefits/Performance Measurements Narrative:
The order cycle — including confirmation — is down to 5 minutes.
The system has resulted in a savings of over $30 million.
Buyers spend more time negotiating and less time on paperwork.
The system has resulted in a reduction in inventory.
Average processing time plummeted from 20 days down to 4 days.
Suppliers decreased from 30,000 to 20,000.
Purchases made on corporate contracts increased from 5% up to 50%.
Non-inspected lots increased to 35% from only 1% before.
On-time deliveries went from 21% (within 14 days) to 65% (within 5 days).

A fashion textile manufacturer, with sales in excess of $1.2 billion formed a cross-functional team to implement multiple vendor management programs.  Changes include consolidation of supplier base by 20%, procurement strategies that reduced inventory and procurement costs, vendor partnerships that couple evaluations with objective performance criteria and actionable improvement plans, and reduction of lead times for targeted materials by 25% to 50%.  This move reduced production lead times, lowered inventories, shortened planning horizons, reduced costs, and increased supplier agility.  Ultimately, the company recognized an overall 50% reduction in product cycle, reduced response time for critical suppliers, and synchronized material supply and production planning process.

Accounts Payable (A/P) Policy/Process (non-EDI)

Best Practice Option
Best practices for A/P processing focus on streamlining manual intensive activities, employing policies that increase incentives for workflow, and cross training employees.

Streamlining Activities
Sort invoices by type
Consolidate of multiple invoices into one payment
Use procurement cards
Outsource freight bills or institute summary billing

Workflow policies
Audit invoices under $500 using a sampling approach, rather than a 100% audit
Use liberal matching tolerances for account payable transactions
Implement a clean-desk policy: all incoming mail is dealt with within 24 hours of receipt
Date-stamp in mail room to establish actual date of receipt on premises
Track the number of incoming invoices in backlog (awaiting entry into A/P system) as a measure of productivity and workflow
Evaluate receipt settlement

Cross-train employees
Train purchasing personnel in accounts payable processes, and vice versa
Monitor vendor performance (cost, quality, delivery)

Examples of organizations who have utilized best practices for accounts payable:

A large automobile manufacturer reviewed the philosophy of their A/P function. It was determined that the purpose was to pay suppliers and not to process invoices. As part of this fundamental change in approach they decided to switch from paying on receipt of the invoice to paying on receipt of the goods. Invoiceless processing was the preferred solution, achieved by the use of technology.  The change in the process has resulted in a 70% reduction in headcount, a reduction in mismatches on purchase orders, and less paper. Naturally enough this state of operation required close liaison with the suppliers.

Seventy percent of Best Practice Retailers have a significantly narrower vendor base operating with less than 1000 vendors.

A large petroleum company introduced credit cards to be used for purchases less than $500. Annualized savings in terms of time and processing costs amounted to $400,000, achieved by eliminating 30,000 purchase requisitions and orders per annum and the matching of over 50,000 invoices per annum.

Accounts Payable Technology

Best Practice Option
Best practices for accounts payable technology focus on eliminating paper and automating traditionally manual activities.  Analysis reveals the following best practices for accounts payable technology:

Eliminate Paper
Electronic invoice registration on receipt of invoice
Electronic approval of invoices, from vendor and buyer
Electronic matching — 3 way
Electronic funds transfer, eliminate all manual checks
Automated check signing facilities or direct credit schedules
Electronic system of preventing duplicate payments
Automatic payment of recurring transactions
Electronic requisitioning, routing, and approval
On-line, real time funds/budget sufficiency checking
System can provide reports on vendor performance, cash forecasting, and check reconciliation

Automate Manual Activities
Integrated purchasing, A/P, and G/L/single vendor file
Electronic notification of receipt of goods
EDI linkage to suppliers, paperless purchase orders
Image paper invoices
Electronic invoice registration on receipt of invoice
Electronic funds transfer, eliminate all manual checks
Paperless requisition-to-check processing
Regular review and analysis of supplier database
Supports real-time, on-line vendor master file which is common to the purchasing and A/P systems to ensure data integrity and eliminate data redundancy
Supports real-time, on-line item master file where frequently purchased items can be assigned an item number, unit price, standard cost, target price, lead time and approved vendor
Automatic chargeback generated from matching process


Examples of organizations that have utilized best practices include:

A multi-national Fortune 500 automobile manufacturer has 5 A/P staff worldwide due to its highly automated processes.

A multi-national Fortune 500 automobile manufacturer reduced it’s A/P staff from 400 to 100 in 8 months by using available technology innovatively, radically redesigning its business processes, and restructuring its organizational structure.

The AP division of a multinational corporation implemented a paperless payables system that eliminated the manual matching of purchase order, receipt documents, and supplier invoices.  72% of all vouchers are processed automatically, paper usage was reduced by 80%, and cost to generate a voucher was reduced by 50%.

A large U.S. manufacturer reduced finance staff by 12% by consolidating A/P and payroll across 32 domestic units and installing a single general ledger system. Their benchmarking revealed that U.S. companies were processing 30,000 invoices per year per accounts payable employee.

A large gasoline company undertook a process value analysis of the payables function in order to reduce the processing cost of invoices. This analysis identified that a significant activity undertaken within the A/P process was the keying in and checking of supplier invoices. To speed up this process they decided to use scanning technology which enabled:
Invoice accuracy to be automatically checked
Keying operators’ time to be removed
Routing of invoices for authorization to the appropriate person to be automatically performed
Removal of paper
Reduction of the time it took to have information available
This company reduced processing costs by 50% and reduced the number of accounting managers by 35%.

A medical center in the U.S. has taken on EDI resulting in an almost 90% paperless hospital office.  As a result, customer service has improved significantly. Insurance claims are now processed using EDI. In addition, customer service representatives are able to answer billing queries immediately since the relevant information can be called up its imaging system rather than having to dig up stacks of records. Also, of importance, is the client end of the system, where PC’s run OS/2 which has a multitasking capability that customer service staff can use to enhance the service they give to patients. Billing inquiries are answered immediately and the quick turnaround on reimbursement claims has improved the hospital’s cashflow.

The Industrial Products Division of a large U.S. corporation implemented a “paperless” payables system that eliminated the manual matching of purchase order, receipt documents, and supplier invoices. This approach, also known as “evaluated receipt settlement”, has been embraced by a number of industries — namely automotive.  Previously, A/P clerks spent their days sorting and matching copies of purchase orders to copies of receipts to invoices and then entering this information into a vouchering system. When there is no good match, the copies are set aside for follow-up research. A/P clerks then track down purchasing agents to confirm price changes and chase down receiving personnel to verify quantities.
      Currently, the new A/P process begins when receiving personnel enter into the system the quantity received at the dock. The system then matches the receipt to a line item on a purchase order, retrieves the unit price, and generates a voucher. In addition to integrating the price file with the purchasing and receiving systems, a number of procedural changes were implemented, for example all purchase orders were required to have the correct price, and all receipts had to be handled in a timely and accurate fashion. That is, the purchase department’s performance must be 100% accurate every time — employees must negotiate price and communicate it. The receiving department’s performance must also be 100% accurate every time — employees must receive items the day they arrive, count them correctly, and communicate them correctly.
