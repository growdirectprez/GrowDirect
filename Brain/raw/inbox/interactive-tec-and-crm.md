### Interactive Technologies and Relationship Marketing Strategies

In recent years, marketing scholars and practitioners have become increasingly interested in a marketing philosophy that is somewhat different from traditional transaction marketing. This philosophy, which we will refer to as "relationship marketing," emphasizes the building and nurturing of long-term relationships with individual customers. The general idea is that if a firm is able to serve customers well, customers will want to develop loyal, long-term relationships with that firm.

The advantages of such relationships are obvious: Customers who have developed loyal relationships with a firm tend to stay with the firm longer, spend more money with the firm, and refer the firm to their friends and family members. And because these customers are already familiar with the firm's products and services, the costs associated with serving them are often lower than those associated with serving new customers.

Traditionally, a number of obstacles have prevented firms from practicing relationship marketing in an effective way. For one thing, relationship marketing requires that firms have a great deal of information about each of their individual customers. Traditionally, acquiring such information has been both time-consuming and expensive. Another obstacle has been the difficulty of actually using customer information to create value; even if a firm has detailed information about its customers, it can be difficult to leverage this information to create offerings that effectively match the needs of individual customers.

Interactive technology, however, is helping to make relationship marketing both more feasible and more powerful. The purpose of this note is to explore the various ways in which interactive technologies are being used to facilitate the practice of relationship marketing.

### Figure 1: Transaction Marketing vs. Relationship Marketing

| | Transaction Marketing | Relationship Marketing |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing Mindset | Make a sale | Keep a customer |
| Information Flow | One-way | Two-way |
| Focus | Product | Customer |
| Performance Measure | Market Share | Share of Customer |
| Goal | Acquire new customers | Retain current customers |
| Tools | Advertising, promotions, discounts | Dialogue, personalization, customization |
| Customer | Anonymous | Known |

### The Relationship Marketing Process (Figure 2)

An iterative cycle:

1. **Interactions** with customers (across multiple touchpoints)
2. **Learning About Customers** (knowledge acquisition)
3. **Customization** of the marketing mix (products, communications, channels, price)
4. More **Interactions** (now personalized) — loop back

### Knowledge Acquisition

Knowledge acquisition is the process by which firms learn about their customers. Traditional methods (surveys, focus groups, secondary research) are being supplemented — and in some cases replaced — by interactive technologies that allow firms to gather information directly from customers in real-time.

**Passive data collection** (clickstream, cookies, log files): Firms can track what customers do on their sites without asking them directly. DoubleClick, for example, places cookies on user computers to track browsing behavior across multiple sites.

**Active data collection** (forms, registrations, surveys): Firms explicitly ask customers to provide information, often in exchange for value (free content, personalized services, discounts).

**MatchLogic** (sidebar): A company that provides services to help firms aggregate customer information from multiple sources — email, web browsing, purchase history — to build more complete customer profiles.

### Privacy and Trust

As firms collect more information about customers, privacy concerns have grown. Two cases are illustrative:

- **Geocities**: Was accused by the FTC of deceptively collecting personal information from children and adults; settled the case in 1998.
- **AOL**: Faced backlash when it disclosed a subscriber's identity to the Navy, leading to his discharge under "don't ask, don't tell." The subscriber sued and won.

**Truste** and similar services offer trust marks to reassure customers that firms are following privacy best practices.

### Customer Differentiation

Not all customers are created equal. Once a firm has acquired knowledge about its customers, it can segment them based on their value to the firm and tailor its marketing efforts accordingly.

### Lifetime Value (Figure 3)

The value of a customer over the entire duration of their relationship with the firm. Components:

- **Acquisition cost** (negative, upfront)
- **Base profit** (ongoing margin)
- **Revenue growth** (customer spends more over time)
- **Cost savings** (customer requires less support, makes fewer mistakes)
- **Referrals** (customer brings in new customers)
- **Price premium** (loyal customers pay more)

The sum of these components, discounted to present value, is the customer's LTV. Firms should invest in customers in proportion to their LTV.

### Mass Customization

The ability to produce customized products at near-mass-production prices. Interactive technology enables mass customization by allowing firms to gather detailed specifications from each customer and then produce the customized product using flexible manufacturing systems.

**Levi's Original Spin**: Customers can go to a Levi's store, have their measurements taken, and order jeans customized to their exact specifications. The jeans are then manufactured and delivered to the customer within a few weeks.

### Customizing Products

Three approaches:

1. **Create**: Allow customers to specify the product from scratch (Levi's, CDuctive, CustomDisc, Dell).
2. **Remember**: Remember what the customer has done or liked in the past and use this to make recommendations (Amazon, Barnes & Noble).
3. **Anticipate**: Predict what the customer will want based on patterns in their behavior and the behavior of similar customers (Firefly, collaborative filtering).

**Firefly** (sidebar): Uses collaborative filtering to make recommendations. The system learns from the ratings of many users and uses this information to predict what any given user will like. Firefly was acquired by Microsoft in 1998.

### Customizing Communications

**Banner Advertising**:
- **DoubleClick**: Serves banner ads targeted to individual users based on their browsing behavior.
- Banner ads can be customized in real-time based on the user's profile.

**Email Marketing**:
- Much cheaper and more targeted than traditional direct mail.
- **Onsale**: Sends daily emails to customers with personalized offers based on their interests.
- Permission-based email (opt-in) is more effective than unsolicited email (spam).

**Experiential Marketing**:
- Creating experiences that engage customers in ways that traditional advertising cannot.
- **Calvin Klein CK One**: Created an interactive web experience that allowed customers to explore the brand in a personalized way.
- **Stolichnaya Vodka**: Used interactive technologies to create experiential marketing campaigns.

**Viral Marketing**:
- Marketing that spreads from customer to customer.
- **Hotmail**: Every email sent through Hotmail included a promotional message at the bottom, turning every user into a marketer. Grew from 0 to 12 million users in 18 months.
- **ICQ**: Similar viral growth through network effects.
- **Amazon Associates**: Pays other websites a commission for referring customers.
- **Yoyodyne** (acquired by Yahoo!): Specialized in viral marketing campaigns.

### Customizing Channels

Interactive technology allows firms to customize how they reach customers and how customers reach them.

**Norwest** (sidebar — counter-example): One of the nation's largest mortgage lenders, fighting the online trend because it has built a 4,000-person sales force and many branch offices. Risks being "Amazon'ed" by start-up firms that have no qualms about bypassing traditional channel structures.

**American Airlines**: Regularly sends email directly to certain customers informing them of special cut-rate fares. This has provided a way for the company to communicate directly with fliers and avoid the costs associated with staffing a toll-free phone bank and paying travel agent commissions. At the same time, American Airlines tickets are still available (albeit at sometimes higher rates) via traditional channels.

### Customizing Price

**Personalized Pricing**: Selling to each user at a different price.

- Airlines (timing, restrictions, travel history)
- **Virtual Vineyards**: Tracks customer behavior in real-time, makes instantaneous special offers.
- Online auctions (eBay, Onsale): Prices set by active customer base dynamics.
- **Priceline.com** (sidebar): Reverse auction. Consumer submits a "conditional purchase offer" with a bid, guarantees with credit card; sellers can fulfill, reject, or counter-offer.

**Versioning**: Offer a product line and let customers choose the version.

- **Wolfram Mathematica**: Student version substantially slower and less expensive than professional — the company incurred additional cost to slow the software down.
- **Intel 386**: Disabled the integrated math coprocessor in one version of the chip to sell a low-priced chip to those who didn't need extra processing power.

### Leveling the Information Playing Field

Customer ignorance – about prices, features, and relative product performance – has traditionally been a source of profit for companies. Today, however, online shopping agents ("bots" or "spiders") can perform automatic price and feature comparisons in a matter of seconds.

Examples: MySimon (~800 merchants), Compare.net, Jango (Excite), Junglee (Amazon), Yahoo! Shopping, C2B (Inktomi).

### Summary: A Learning Relationship

> A Learning Relationship between a customer and an enterprise gets smarter and smarter with each individual interaction, defining in ever more detail the customer's own individual needs and tastes.
>
> — Peppers, D., and Rogers, M. (1997), *Enterprise One to One*, Doubleday, p. 15.

The learning works in both directions: The firm develops a knowledge base about each customer; the customer develops a deep awareness of the irreplicability of the value offered by the firm. Both parties' interest to continue the relationship, and it becomes difficult for another firm to duplicate the level of personalization inherent in the products and services offered.

## Notes for synthesis

Key vocabulary the 1999 academic frame used that the Third Branch thesis inherits:

- **Share of Customer** (vs Market Share) — the KPI flip that anchored relationship marketing
- **Learning Relationship** (Peppers & Rogers, 1997) — the mechanism for lock-in via accumulated personalization
- **Markets-of-One** — the logical endpoint of mass customization
- **Transaction → Relationship** — the philosophical shift
- **Create / Remember / Anticipate** — the trilogy of product customization
- **Banner / Email / Experiential / Viral** — the taxonomy of communication customization
- **Personalized Pricing / Versioning** — the taxonomy of price customization

All of these predate — and precisely describe — what BroadVision was selling into American Airlines, Hallmark, Le Shop, RS Components, Vodafone, Guess?, MUJI, Kenneth Cole, and Swarovski in 1998–2002. And all of it failed to reach substrate: the tracking was on the channel (cookies, clickstream), not on the goods.

The Third Branch thesis: the 1999 academic vocabulary was right. The substrate was wrong. Agents + observation fix the substrate. The vocabulary finally gets to do its job.
