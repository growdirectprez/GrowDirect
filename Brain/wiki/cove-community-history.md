# Community History

The WPBCA community at Abalone Cove sits on land shaped by five distinct eras — each leaving legal, cultural, and physical traces still visible today.

## Tongva Origins (~5100 BCE)

The Tongva people established the Chowigna village at Abalone Cove approximately 7,100 years ago. Shell middens (ancient abalone shell layers) still exist at the site. A Tongva monument was erected at Abalone Cove Park in 2021.

## Rancho Era (1882-1913)

Jotham Bixby acquired 16,000+ acres of Rancho de los Palos Verdes in 1882. In 1913, Frank Vanderlip Sr. — one of the architects of the Federal Reserve, fresh from the 1910 Jekyll Island meeting — purchased the entire peninsula from the Bixby syndicate.

The Bixby connection runs deeper: Fred H. Bixby also purchased El Cojo Ranch (adjacent to Hollister Ranch, Santa Barbara) in 1912. Two branches of the same family, two stretches of contested California coastline, same 50+ year access fight still playing out today.

## Japanese American Farming (1906-1942)

Beginning in 1906, Kumekichi Ishibashi pioneered Japanese American farming at Portuguese Bend. By the late 1930s, 200+ families were farming the peninsula. Executive Order 9066 ended that in 1942. The families were sent to internment camps. Only five returned. The Hatano Farm, the last Japanese American farm on the peninsula, was evicted in 2021. Pre-1942 tomato genetics still grow as volunteers in neighborhood yards — living agricultural heritage from seeds that predate the war.

## PV Corp and WPBCA (1925-1952)

In 1925, the Vanderlip syndicate reorganized as Palos Verdes Corporation (Delaware). Hired the Olmsted Brothers (same firm that designed Central Park's successor projects) to plan the entire peninsula. Between April 1949 and November 1952, PV Corp recorded six instruments creating WPBCA and Tract 14649. See [[Brain/wiki/cove-legal-framework|Cove Legal Framework]] for the full chain.

Frank Vanderlip Jr. sold all PV Corp stock to Great Lakes Carbon Corporation in 1953 for ~$9M. The family retained 500 acres in Portuguese Bend.

## The Shore Club Era (1929-1972)

The Abalone Shore Club operated from 1929. Ray Wallace, a Disney Imagineer, illustrated its pamphlet. Edna M. Hall captured the community in "Cottages by the Sea."

**The 1972 Vote:** In June 1972, the community faced the same choice it faces today. Karl Rodi (Beverly Hills attorney) proposed 138-170 condos on Portuguese Point. Dick Fitzgerald (LA County Dept of Beaches) proposed a shoreline park. Dick Karshner, President of the Abalone Shore Club, organized the vote. The community chose the park. Abalone Cove Shoreline Park exists today because of that vote. Fifty-four years later, the same question: condos or preservation.

## City Incorporation (1973)

RPV incorporated in 1973 — 24 years after the PV Corp declarations. The city exists because communities like WPBCA decided to incorporate to stop condo developers. That is literally why RPV was created. And now the same city is rezoning for condos.

### First-Person Account: Marilyn Ryan

Marilyn Ryan moved to Rancho Palos Verdes in 1967, became active in the local League of Women Voters, ran for City Council, won, became Mayor, and signed the 1975 RPV General Plan + EIR. She was then elected to the California State Assembly (1976-1982), where she was directly involved in implementing **Proposition 20 (1972)** and the **California Coastal Act of 1976**.

Her UCLA / California State Archives oral history (interviews November 2000 + January-March 2001) is the best primary-source narrative for the political environment in which WPBCA's covenants operated through the incorporation transition. See [[coac-1975-general-plan-eir]] for the policy document she signed and [`Cove/docs/archive/originals/people/2000-2001-marilyn-ryan-oral-history-ucla.md`](../../Cove/docs/archive/originals/people/2000-2001-marilyn-ryan-oral-history-ucla.md) for the full annotated source. Quote on the landslide: *"One of the problems with the peninsula is there's a landslide and you can't… It's very difficult to get…"*
