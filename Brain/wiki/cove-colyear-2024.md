# Colyear v. Rolling Hills Community Association (2024)

California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District, Division Four, Case B308382. Filed March 1, 2024. Certified for partial publication.

This is the most recent authoritative appellate decision on the Palos Verdes Corporation declaration scheme. It rewrites the analysis for whether Declaration 100 applies to 0 Clipper Road.

## The Short Version

Richard Colyear owns property in Rolling Hills covered by **Declaration 150-M** (1944), an annexation declaration under PVC's master **Declaration 150** (1936). Declaration 150 contains a tree-trimming covenant that allows the Association to enter any property and cut trees to preserve views. Declaration 150-M does not contain that covenant.

The Rolling Hills Community Association tried to enforce the Tree CC against Colyear anyway, arguing that:
1. Declaration 150 was intended to cover all of Rolling Hills as a General Plan
2. Declaration 150-M references Declaration 150
3. Kelvin Vanderlip testified about PVC's intent
4. *Russell v. Palos Verdes Properties* (1963) supported the General Plan theory

The Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court's ruling for Colyear. **The Tree CC does not apply because:**
- Declaration 150 does not describe Colyear's property
- Declaration 150-M does not clearly and unequivocally incorporate Declaration 150
- Extrinsic evidence of PVC's intent is insufficient without proper recordation
- *Russell's* commentary on the General Plan was only dicta
- Constructive notice requires proper recording in the chain of title

## The Kelvin Vanderlip Admission

The case includes the single most devastating piece of testimony about the PVC scheme. In November 1947, Vanderlip (PVC president and Frank Vanderlip's son) admitted in writing that:

> "Practically all of the provisions which were contained in Declaration No. 150, ... were removed by our attorneys as far back as the Flying Triangle Declaration [(Declaration 160)] for the purpose of simplification."

PVC's own attorneys stripped provisions from later declarations. On purpose. Starting with Declaration 160 (the Flying Triangle, 1939). The master General Plan was undermined by the same family that created it.

## Why This Matters for Declaration 100 / 0 Clipper

Declaration 100 is to Lot H what Declaration 150 is to Rolling Hills — the master general plan recorded by PVC as the framework for subsequent tracts.

Under *Colyear*, Declaration 100 probably does NOT apply to 0 Clipper Road because:

| Requirement (per *Colyear/Citizens*) | Status for 0 Clipper |
|---|---|
| Master declaration describes the property | Page 155 smeared — cannot verify |
| Implementing declaration references master | Uncertain |
| Implementing declaration incorporates master clearly | Uncertain |
| Chain of title references master | No (per CTC title report) |
| Joint intent between grantor and grantee | Not demonstrable |

The CTC title report for APN 7573-006-024 shows the chain referencing only Tract 43725 (the 1986 reversion). No Declaration 100. No Declaration 101. No 1950 Lot H Declaration.

## The User's Instinct Was Correct

The question asked during discovery: "what does it mean if declaration 100 arises from nowhere i dont think it is enforecable on clipper."

*Colyear* confirms this instinct. Declaration 100's substantive provisions probably cannot be enforced against 0 Clipper Road under current California appellate law. Even if the property description on page 155 covers the whole of Lot H (which fragments suggest), the court requires:
1. The restriction to be in the chain of title OR
2. A clear, unequivocal incorporation by reference in the annexation declaration

Neither is present for 0 Clipper.

## What *Colyear* Does NOT Touch

*Colyear* narrows the private covenant argument. It leaves intact:

- **Coastal Specific Plan (1978)** — municipal regulations, not private covenants
- **1980 Wong Subdivision approval** — the city's own application of the Plan to this parcel
- **Woodring 1946** — federal landslide documentation
- **Albers v. County (1965)** — inverse condemnation liability
- **LRPMP (2014)** — the city's own admission of CC&Rs on adjacent Parcel 106 land
- **Phillippe v. Shapell (1987)** — the geology, not covenants, that stopped the 1973 Filiorum sale
- **Monks v. RPV (2008)** — landslide moratorium framework
- **Russell's core holding** — equitable enforcement against transferees with notice (still good law)

## The Narrative Implication

The article's existing frame — "The system designed to protect it doesn't work unless someone does the homework" — is now supported by an appellate court opinion from last year.

*Colyear* documents what the article already argues:
- PVC created a grand vision for protective restrictions
- Their own attorneys simplified it away
- The declarations that actually got recorded are inconsistent
- Roland Swaffield documented the "wide divergence" of restrictions in 1947
- Courts in 2024 will not enforce what wasn't properly recorded

The private covenant layer is compromised BY DESIGN. That's not the article's position — that's the appellate court's finding. The Vanderlips intended uniformity; their attorneys broke it.

This strengthens the article's thesis that **the public regulatory framework is what matters** — the Coastal Specific Plan, the landslide moratorium, the ESA, the geology. The private restrictions are in doubt. The public record is not.

## The Filiorum Name Link

Related: the *Phillippe v. Shapell* (1987) case established that a 1973 attempt to buy Filiorum Corporation land failed "due to geological problems." The Filiorum Corporation is the entity in the 1930 grant deed (Book 10226, Page 170) that incorporated Declarations 100 and 101. The same corporation whose name is now on PVPLC's 191-acre Filiorum Reserve.

Shapell walked away from Filiorum land because of geology. Fifty-one years later, Clipper Development bought adjacent land. Different answer. Same geology.
