Distribution and Transportation Process Overview
Process Scope and Description:
Receiving: Receiving represents the point of first receipt processing of products whether it is a distribution center, store or a third party facility.
Load Planning and Scheduling: The process uses existing and projected orders and processing required to determine workload over short and intermediate intervals. Using workloads and resources available, short interval schedules are developed for processing and delivery.
Warehouse Management: The process includes the management of space within the distribution center, assignment of product to that space and the maintenance of inventory in that space. The process incorporates putaways, letdowns/replenishment, moves and consolidations.
Merchandise Processing: The process includes all modes of flow of product including cross dock, flow through and picking from stock as pallets, full case and break pack.
Shipping: The process includes planning, execution and monitoring of shipments from DC
s to stores. In addition, it includes tracking and control of shipments to stores from vendors and third parties.
Transportation Management: The process includes the planning, execution and monitoring of all company-controlled transportation in support of both outbound shipments to stores and inbound from vendors.
Import Management: The process incorporates both the product and logistics management components of direct importing. In product management, it includes controlling production specifications and monitoring production and quality. In logistics management, it includes managing third party logistics services, monitoring pipeline flow and monitoring service performance. The process facilitates the accurate costing of product landed at the retailers point of first receipt.
Vendor Order Management: This is the process by which suggested orders from any of the order generation processes are adjusted for minimums, pack size, unit loads, discount or allowance levels, transportation limits (e.g. truck load rounding) and pick up dates before being reviewed and approved as PO
Store Order Management: This is the process by which store replenishment orders and allocations are adjusted for pack, unit loads and transportation limits.

Merchandise Processing
The process includes all modes of flow of product including cross dock, flow through and picking from stock as pallets, full case and break pack.
Item / vendor attributes and the PO determine the mode of flow through the DC for the receipt.
Mode of flow should be driven by logistics planning based on a number of factors, including:
Total supply chain economics and cycle time
Vendor reliability
Vendor lead time
Inventory productivity and carrying cost trade off vs. DC processing cost
Peak capacity limits of one or more modes of flow.
For purposes of consistency in describing processing, cross dock is defined as a mode of flow where a receipt can be completely distributed to an outbound load (live load or staged for loading) without intermediate storage. Cross docking typically involves palletized product. 
Flow through requires some transient storage in order to supply product to cover a complete shipping cycle.
Cross docking and flow through are the lowest DC cost modes but require vendor reliability and close coordination of inbound shipments to make them totally effective modes of flow.
Flow through break pack is used to address the need for store inventory productivity, when there is a high level vendor reliability and coordination of transportation.
Picking from stock addresses the need for continuous supply to support customer service (in-stock) and to optimize the deployment of internal pipeline inventory.

Transportation Managements
The process includes the planning, execution and monitoring of all company-controlled transportation in support of both outbound shipments to stores and inbound from vendors.
Best practices in transportation management are determined by:
Degree of retailer control over the fleet and drivers
Degree to which outbound and inbound can be tied together. The degree to which they can be tied together depends on:
Vendor location vs. store location
The mix of control is typically:
Owned/leased
Contract
Preferred (next tier)
All third party is an alternative.
The recommendations are based on the assumption that there will be a typical mix of control rather than all third party service. 
It is assumed that inbound will be tied into store delivery when it is cost-effective to do so. When it is not, pick ups will be scheduled independent of store delivery
Inbound tied to store delivery requires routing capabilities. Routing and scheduling must consider the same service criteria that would be placed on a vendor for prepaid transportation, i.e., keeping the transportation component of lead time as short as possible in order to facilitate maintaining lower inventory and keeping deliveries reliable in order to maintain in stock customer service level targets.
It is assumed that Staples will continue to convert prepaid vendors to collect and inbound consolidation will be used for lower volume products.
Transportation Management (Continued)n
The use of tracking depends on the flow of inbound. Typically any points of consolidation or deconsolidation would utilize electronic transportation/order(s) tracking.
Inbound with contract carriers with a drop trailer program should interface with yard management. The transportation management component of yard management primarily focuses on accounting for the carriers
Outbound is routed based on delivery only or integrated with backhaul. Depending upon variation in volume over time, routing can be relatively stable or may need to be evaluated every shipping cycle based on existing and projected workload.
Longer term planning is driven by the logistics plan and the merchandise plan.
The logistics plan should drive the transportation resource and capacity planning.
If the fleet is owned or leased to the retailer, an equipment management process is needed which includes the following functions:
Mileage tracking
Maintenance control
Utilization
Incident reporting
Equipment management ties into fixed asset management if it is owned.p

In logistics, it includes monitoring production and quality managing third party logistics services, monitoring pipeline flow and monitoring service performance. The process facilitates the accurate costing of product landed at the point of first receipt. This process definition covers the logistics components only.
The process is driven by production agreements, PO
s and letters of credit (LC
Depending upon the product and the reliability of the manufacturer, production monitoring and quality monitoring should take place. Again depending on conditions, the monitoring can occur through agents.
Anticipated release of product triggers the payment of the letter of credit in the case of direct impacts and triggers inland freight and consolidation arrangements.
Timing of product release and quality inspection results provide input to compliance monitoring for the manufacturer.
Inland freight can be arranged by the 
Container consolidation may be done:
Preplanned (through order management)
Done by the consolidator
Multiple country consolidation should be done when possible.
Consolidation customs clearance and preparation for shipment (by whatever means) is the next step. Tracking should be done to:
Project product arrivals
Provide input for measuring service
Leading 3rd party provider of freight forwarding and customs brokerage firms offer electronic tracking input as a normal part of their service. (It must link into a Staples tracking capability)
Similarly, ocean and air freight carriers provide electronic tracking services.
US Customs clearance may be handled by a 3rd party or managed directly. US Customs itself provides an EDI link for tracking.
Import Management - Logistics (Continued)s
Note that in the future when it it is more advantageous to do so, Staples will deal more directly with import service providers.
Inbound shipment from the port of entry in the US to the DC is handled by domestic transportation management assuming that it is set up to manage inbound freight cost - effectively. If there is interim consolidation, tracking should be done.
Inbound tracking at foreign customs and US customs and with inbound carriers is done to:
Project product arrivals
Provide support to assess performance for that component of the inbound pipeline
Depending upon the quality and reliability of the manufacturer, post receipt quality control may be required. If product is rejected as a result of post receipt quality control, then it needs to be located (using Warehouse Management) and in DC inventory and stock status / ledger.
