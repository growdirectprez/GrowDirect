## 1. The Historical Parallel — IP Address Allocation

### 1.1 What Happened

In the early 1990s, the American Registry for Internet Numbers (ARIN) and its predecessor organizations allocated IP address blocks to organizations that requested them. A /8 block (16.7 million addresses) could be obtained for a trivial administrative fee. The addresses were just numbers. Nobody outside a small technical community understood what they would become.

By 2011, IPv4 address space was exhausted. ARIN issued its last free allocation in 2015. By 2021, the market price for IPv4 addresses reached $50–60 per address. Organizations that obtained /8 blocks in the 1990s — MIT, Ford, the U.S. Department of Defense, Hewlett-Packard — held assets worth $500M–$1B per block at market rates.

The asset was not the number. The asset was the position in the namespace — claimed early, when the cost was negligible and the utility was speculative.

### 2.2 The Structural Claim

The investment argument rests on four pillars, each of which has a direct analog in the IP allocation history:

**Pillar 1: Positional scarcity within abundance.**
IPv4 addresses are a finite set (4.3 billion). But the scarcity that created value was not the total supply — it was the positional scarcity of established allocations. A /8 block registered in 1993 carried routing table presence, institutional recognition, and network integration that a newly purchased address in 2020 could not replicate.

Ordinals are similarly abundant in total — anyone can inscribe new ones. But the Genesis Pool carries protocol provenance (the first canonical notarization schema inscribed on the chain), temporal position (the earliest block heights in the notarization namespace), and founder origin (minted from the founder's own mining reward). These properties exist at specific block heights. They cannot be replicated at later block heights. The scarcity is positional, not absolute.

**Pillar 2: Protocol authority without a central registry.**
IP addresses required ARIN to assign authority. The Ordinals namespace has no registry — and that is the point. Authority derives from the protocol itself: the range that inscribed the canonical notarization schema first, at the earliest block height, with the most coherent protocol definition, owns the namespace by precedent.

This is how standards actually emerge in decentralized systems. TCP/IP was not mandated. It won by being first, being open, and being adopted. GrowDirect's notarization protocol establishes the same dynamic: first canonical schema, earliest timestamp, open specification, network effect.

**Pillar 3: The timestamp as filing date.**
In patent law, the filing date establishes priority. In the IP allocation history, the registration date established precedence. On the Bitcoin time chain, the block height is the filing date — and it is the most trustless filing date ever created.

GrowDirect's Genesis Pool inscriptions carry block heights that are permanently earlier than any competitor's. A competitor can mint their own ordinals, inscribe their own protocol, and claim their own namespace. They cannot inscribe at a block height that has already been mined. The chain is the authority. The timestamp is the precedent.

**Pillar 4: Compounding utility creates escape velocity.**
Early IP allocations became more valuable as the internet grew — not because the numbers changed, but because the infrastructure built on those numbers compounded. Routing tables, DNS records, peering agreements, and application dependencies all reinforced the positional value.

## 3. Anticipated Counterarguments — Addressed

### 3.1 "Ordinals are infinite. Anyone can mint more. IP space was finite by design."

This is the most common objection and the most superficially compelling. It is also wrong in the dimension that matters.

IPv4 scarcity was absolute (4.3B addresses, hard cap). Ordinal scarcity is positional. The supply of new ordinals is indeed unlimited — every new Bitcoin block creates new satoshis with new ordinal numbers. But the value proposition is not "we own ordinals." It is "we own the earliest canonical notarization protocol inscribed at the earliest block heights, with provenance tracing to the founder's own mining reward."

No amount of new ordinal minting can replicate that position. You can always mint new ordinals. You cannot go back and mint at the block height where the Genesis Pool was established. You cannot retroactively earn the mining reward that funded it. You cannot rewrite the protocol inscription timestamps.

The analog: IPv6 provides effectively unlimited addresses. IPv4 allocations from the 1990s are still worth billions. Abundance of the general resource does not eliminate the value of established positions within it.

### 3.2 "There is no IANA equivalent. No authority assigns Ordinal ranges."

Correct. And that is the strongest feature of the system, not a weakness.

IANA worked because the internet needed centralized coordination in its early phase. The Bitcoin time chain does not need a registry because the chain itself is the registry. Block height is the timestamp. Key custody is the proof of control. The inscription content is the protocol definition.

In decentralized systems, authority is not assigned. It is established by precedent, adoption, and network effect. The first canonical schema, at the earliest block height, with the widest adoption, becomes the standard — not because an authority declared it, but because the network converged on it.

GrowDirect is not asking for permission to own a namespace. It is establishing one. The chain records the fact. The market decides the value.

### 3.3 "What stops someone from forking the protocol and minting their own range?"

Nothing. And that is also fine.

Anyone can fork TCP/IP. Anyone can create a new DNS root. Anyone can build a competing internet protocol. The question is not "can it be forked" but "does the fork carry the same authority?"

A fork of the El Jeffe notarization protocol carries none of the following:

- **Provenance:** The forked protocol was not inscribed at Bitcoin block 884,201. It was inscribed later. The chain proves it.
- **History:** The forked protocol has zero notarized events. GrowDirect's range has a growing canonical record from day one.
- **Founder origin:** The forked protocol was not funded from a proof-of-work mining reward with an unbroken UTXO lineage. It was funded from a market purchase. The provenance story is commercially manufactured, not organically earned.
- **Network effect:** Every merchant, every validator, every auditor already pointed at the GrowDirect range must be convinced to switch. The switching cost is the accumulated history they would be abandoning.

Forks are noise. The canonical range is signal.
